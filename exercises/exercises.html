Exercises

Three short exercises, one per big idea from the session. Do them after running the session do-files through 03_generate_variables.do, so that data/simulated/wage_survey_clean.dta exists.

Worked solutions: solutions/solutions.do, but try each task yourself first.

Exercise 1: The trap, one more time

task 1 is the in-class drill after the break; task 2 is yours

Using wage_survey_clean.dta:

  1. Count how many respondents have 9 or more years of schooling, first the naive way, then correctly. How big is the difference, and why is it exactly that number?
  2. Among rural, childhood-poor respondents only, how many are missing schooling? Compare that to the missing count among urban, never-poor respondents. What do you notice, and why does it matter for research? (Hint: if data are missing more often for some groups, dropping them quietly changes who your results are about.)

Exercise 2: Build variables without stepping on the rake

after segment 4

  1. Create an indicator tertiary equal to 1 for respondents with 13 or more years of schooling, 0 otherwise, making sure the 200 missing-schooling respondents end up missing, not 0 or 1. Verify with tabulate tertiary, missing.
  2. Using egen, create prov_p50 = the median hourly wage in each respondent's province. Which province has the highest median wage?
  3. Give tertiary a variable label and value labels ("No tertiary" / "Tertiary"), and confirm they show up in tabulate.

Exercise 3: Your first heterogeneity result

after segment 5

  1. Run the Mincer regression (with the segment-5 controls) separately for women and for men. Which group has the higher estimated return to schooling?
  2. Now estimate the difference directly with an interaction (c.schooling##i.female). Is the difference statistically significant at the 5 percent level?
  3. In one sentence each: (i) interpret the interaction coefficient in percent-per-year terms; (ii) say why this is an association, not a causal effect. (If the second sentence hurts, read the caveat section of the README, then Module C.)
