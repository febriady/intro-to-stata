4But first, those ages

Nobody in a wage survey is 320 years old. Draw a histogram of age, then list the people with an age above 100 together with their year of birth. What happened, and what can you do about it?

Show solution
histogram age
list id age birthyr if age > 100

The histogram is one crushed bar: nearly everyone is squeezed to the left because a few values are enormous. A picture shows a wrong value before any table does. The list gives ten people, aged 150 to 320. Now look at the birth year next to each one. The survey was run in 2014, so someone born in 1994 is 20, not 200. Every one of the ten ages was typed with an extra zero. The file itself tells you the right value, so repair it instead of throwing it away:

replace age = 2014 - birthyr if age > 100
summarize age

Stata reports 10 real changes made, and ages now run from 15 to 35. Setting the ten to missing, replace age = . if age > 100, would also have worked. It would have cost you ten people in every regression that uses age.

5What does a typical worker earn

"What does a typical worker earn?"

Report the mean, the median, and the 25th and 75th percentiles of the hourly wage. Which number answers the question?

Show solution
summarize hwage, detail

Mean 11,218 Rupiah an hour; median 8,571; the middle half earn between 5,189 and 13,813. A few high earners pull the mean up, so the median is what a typical worker earns. Report the median, and say that it is the median. At the 2014 exchange rate it is about half a euro an hour.

6Share who finished secondary school, by background

"What share finished secondary school, and is that share lower for people who grew up poor?"

Create a variable high equal to 1 for people with 12 or more years of schooling and 0 otherwise. Verify it with tabulate high, missing. Is it correct? Then cross it with cpoor.

Show solution

The obvious version:

generate high = schooling >= 12
tabulate high, missing

The table shows 5,220 zeros and 2,780 ones, and no missing values. That is wrong. Two hundred people never answered the schooling question, yet every one of them ended up with a 1.

Why. Stata stores a missing value as larger than any number, so . >= 12 is true. Every > or >= on a variable that can be missing needs & !missing() after it.

It is a Stata rule, not a law of nature. In SAS a missing value is smaller than any number, so there the same mistake hides in schooling < 12 instead. In R a comparison with NA gives NA, and what happens next depends on the function you hand it to. In a new program, check what it does with a missing value before you trust any comparison.

The correct version, then the cross-table:

drop high
generate high = schooling >= 12 if !missing(schooling)
tabulate high, missing
tabulate cpoor high, row

Now 5,220 zeros, 2,580 ones, and 200 missing, as it should be. The row percentages answer the question: 38 percent of people who did not grow up poor finished secondary school, against 21 percent of those who did. Both shares are computed on the 7,800 who answered.

7The return to a year of schooling, with controls

"What is the return to a year of schooling once we hold province, gender, age and location fixed?"

Create the log wage. Then regress it on years of schooling, female, urban, age, and a dummy for every province, using factor-variable notation for the provinces: i.province.

Show solution
generate lhwage = ln(hwage)
regress lhwage schooling female urban age i.province

Number of obs: 7,800. The 200 without a schooling answer are silently left out, and the N is the only place it shows. The coefficient on schooling is 0.079 (standard error 0.002). A year of schooling is associated with an 8 percent higher wage, holding the controls fixed. i.province turned one variable into twelve dummies, with the first province left out as the comparison. R-squared is 0.17.

If you skipped task 4, the coefficient on age is meaningless: ten people aged 150 to 320 would be sitting in it.

8Did the model behave in class if time allows

After the regression, generate the predicted values and the residuals, called yhat and uhat. Compute the average of both, and check their N against the regression's N.

Show solution
predict yhat
predict uhat, residuals
summarize yhat uhat

predict works from the regression Stata has in memory, so run it right after regress. Without an option it gives the fitted value; , residuals gives actual minus fitted.

The residuals average zero to many decimals. That is arithmetic, not a sign that the model is good: OLS chooses the coefficients that make it so. The predicted values average 9.05, the mean of the log wage. Both have 7,800 observations, the same as the regression. The only people without a prediction are the 200 without a schooling answer.

9One number for the relationship in class if time allows

Compute the correlation coefficient between the hourly wage and years of schooling. What does it tell you that the regression does not, and the reverse?

Show solution
correlate hwage schooling

0.34, on 7,800 observations. A correlation is one number for the whole cloud, with no units and nothing held fixed. It tells you the two move together; the regression in task 7 tells you by how much, and with what else held constant.

10Different for people who grew up poor?

"And does that return look different for people who grew up poor?"

Run the regression from task 7 again, only for people who grew up poor (cpoor == 1). Compare the coefficient on schooling with task 7.

Show solution
regress lhwage schooling female urban age i.province if cpoor == 1

Number of obs: 2,166. The coefficient on schooling is 0.060 (standard error 0.004), against 0.079 for everyone. In this sample a year of schooling is associated with a smaller wage gain for people who grew up poor. Whether that difference is causal is a question for another course. That a regression lets you ask it is the point of today.
